A citizen movement for better everyday life

We don't want a perfect city.
We want a city that works.

We pay taxes. We follow rules. We raise our families here. We should not have to accept broken roads, unsafe streets, dirty air, flooded homes and endless complaints as “normal”.

This is not about blaming a city. It is about refusing to believe that bad civic life is normal.
01 — The everyday problem

You shouldn't need a VIP pass to live with dignity.

A city is not successful because it has big buildings, flyovers and new apps. It is successful when an ordinary person can walk home safely, breathe reasonably clean air, find clean water and get a broken public service fixed.

“Just be careful.”
Why is that the solution?
“Everyone faces it.”
That doesn't make it okay.
02 — What we are asking for

11 basic things a city owes you.

Not luxury. Not VIP treatment. The minimum conditions for a safe, healthy and dignified life.

03 — This is a movement

Less complaining.
More demanding. More measuring. More doing.

We don't need another angry WhatsApp group. We need citizens who know what to demand, evidence that proves what is failing, and a public system that cannot quietly ignore it.

01 · See it

Spot a civic problem in your street, neighbourhood or city.

02 · Document it

Photos, location, dates, data. Turn frustration into evidence.

03 · Demand it

Ask for a responsible authority, a deadline and a measurable outcome.

04 · Track it

Follow up. Publish the result. Celebrate what works. Challenge what doesn't.

04 — The idea

Turn “please fix this” into a civic right.

The proposed Right to Live framework connects every civic right to a government duty, a measurable standard and accountability when the standard is repeatedly missed.

RIGHT

A person deserves a defined civic condition.

DUTY

Someone in government must own the responsibility.

STANDARD

The service has a measurable time and quality target.

ACCOUNTABILITY

Repeated failure has an escalation and public record.

06 — Your city. Your voice.

Don't just say “someone should do something.”
Be the someone.

Start with one street. One problem. One piece of evidence. Then find ten people who care. Movements don't begin with millions. They begin when ordinary people decide that “good enough” isn't good enough anymore.
